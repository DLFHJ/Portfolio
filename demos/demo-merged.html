01

The Challenge

Public spaces are increasingly mediated by digital systems — yet most of those systems are designed for efficiency rather than experience. Wayfinding is utilitarian. Information is generic. The emotional and social dimensions of moving through a city are almost entirely ignored.

This project asked a different question: what if public interfaces could respond to the rhythms, moods, and needs of the people around them — rather than simply broadcasting static information?

02

Research

Field research at three urban locations combined observation with structured interviews. Over three weeks, we mapped pedestrian flows, dwell times, and moments of hesitation — building a detailed picture of how people actually navigate shared space versus how designers assume they do.

Left: annotated observation maps from the Hauptplatz site visit. Right: interview session with a long-term resident documenting habitual navigation patterns.

03

Design

Three rounds of prototyping explored different interaction models. The first used proximity sensing to adjust ambient sound; the second introduced visual feedback through floor projection; the third combined both with real-time crowd density data pulled from transport APIs.

Each prototype was tested in-situ with 8–12 participants using a think-aloud protocol. Iterations were rapid — a one-week cycle from concept sketch to deployed test.

Prototype 3 installed in the Hauptplatz underpass. Ambient light intensity adapts in real time to crowd density, creating a subtle but legible spatial signal.

Detail of the floor projection system during the Hauptplatz pilot.

04

Outcome

Post-pilot surveys across 87 respondents showed a measurable increase in perceived safety and comfort in the tested locations. Navigation errors dropped by 34%. More significantly, dwell time in the underpass — previously avoided by most pedestrians after 20:00 — increased by over 60%.

The project demonstrated that ambient, behaviour-responsive interfaces can shift how people experience urban space — without requiring explicit attention or interaction. The work has been submitted for inclusion in the 2025 Urban Design Austria programme.
